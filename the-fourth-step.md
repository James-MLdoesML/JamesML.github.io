# The 4th step

Last week, OpenAI released [722 mathematical manuscripts, organised into 372 families of results](https://github.com/openai/math), in a public GitHub repository. Many address longstanding open problems. The scale of the release—and the possibility that many of these results may survive expert scrutiny—brought back two memories.

The first was of training for the National Olympiad in Informatics in 2016. The second was of learning Pólya’s four steps for solving a problem.

I was never particularly good at Olympiads. Partly, this was because I was largely self-taught and had little access to proper training. But perhaps I simply was not gifted enough. I did, however, have a knack for making bold predictions.

In 2016, during my final year of high school, I confidently declared that AI would one day solve competitive-programming problems—and even Mathematics Olympiad questions. I hoped, of course, that I might help build such a system. My reasoning was simple: these problems contained recurring patterns. If those patterns could be recognised, recombined, and extended, then this kind of problem-solving might be amenable to machine intelligence.

My friend, sitting beside me on the bus and enduring the ravings of a sore loser, must have thought I was completely delusional. He was not the first person to think so, and he would certainly not be the last.

It is now clear that I won the bet.

But at what cost?

A more hopeful line of thought emerged from my second memory: encountering George Pólya’s approach to problem-solving for the first time as a high-school student. It was a simple but useful framework, and one that my school’s mathematics department emphasised:

1. *Understand the problem.*
2. *Devise a plan.*
3. *Carry out the plan.*
4. *Look back.*

These steps seem almost too obvious to be useful. Yet their simplicity conceals something encouraging: solving problems is not merely an expression of intelligence. It is a skill—one that can be examined, practised, and improved. You, too, can become a better problem-solver.

The fourth step is the one that interests me most. Pólya asks us to pause after finding a solution and reflect: What did we do? Why did it work? What failed along the way? Could the argument be improved? Where else might the same idea be useful?

This is the step I believe we too often neglect, both in mathematical education and in the way mathematical achievements are presented. We celebrate the final proof while concealing the reflections, failed approaches, conceptual revisions, and philosophical questions that made the proof meaningful. Yet “looking back” has often produced some of mathematics’ most important conceptual breakthroughs.

Consider the eighteenth-century debate over the vibrating string.

In 1747, Jean le Rond d’Alembert formulated the wave equation for a vibrating string and found a general solution in terms of travelling waves. Leonhard Euler then broadened the class of curves that might count as valid solutions. Daniel Bernoulli then proposed something remarkable: the motion of a vibrating string could be expressed as a sum of simple harmonic oscillations—a fundamental frequency together with its higher harmonics.

This provoked a serious controversy. Could an arbitrary shape really be represented by an infinite sum of sine waves? What kinds of objects should count as functions? In what sense did such an infinite series converge to the curve it supposedly represented?

Decades later, Joseph Fourier made an even bolder use of trigonometric series in his study of heat diffusion. The same objections returned. Mathematicians such as Lagrange were unconvinced that such series could represent arbitrary functions.

What began as disagreement over the solutions to a physical equation became a much deeper investigation. Mathematicians were forced to ask what a function really was, what it meant for a sequence or series to converge, and what precisely was meant by continuity and integration. Those questions helped drive the nineteenth-century development of real analysis: Riemann’s work on integration, Weierstrass’s rigorous treatment of limits, and eventually parts of Cantor’s work on sets and infinity.

One equation had been solved. But the attempt to understand what its solutions *meant* opened an entire research programme—one that continues today.

Thinking about this made me hopeful. If AI relieves mathematicians of some of the burden of producing proofs, perhaps it will give more people time to perform Pólya’s fourth step. Perhaps more mathematicians will ask what these solutions mean, why certain approaches work, which hidden connections they reveal, and what new concepts are needed to understand them. Proofs generated at scale might not bring mathematical thought to an end. They might supply raw material for a new period of conceptual development.

Then I opened X.

The flood of commentary from non-mathematicians disheartened me again—not because of what it suggested about mathematics, but because of what it revealed about people’s misconceptions of the subject.

One common misconception is that mathematics consists entirely of proofs. Another is that failing to prove a conjecture means that the attempt was useless, or that the concepts developed along the way have no value.

Terence Tao addressed the first misconception years before the arrival of modern large language models in his essay, [*There’s More to Mathematics Than Rigour and Proofs*](https://terrytao.wordpress.com/career-advice/theres-more-to-mathematics-than-rigour-and-proofs/). I will not repeat his argument here, except to recommend that everyone read it.

The second misconception is worth confronting through another story: the emergence of ideals in algebra.

An ideal in ring theory generalises certain familiar features of the integers. The modern theory of ideals grew partly out of nineteenth-century attempts to prove Fermat’s Last Theorem.

Some of those attempts tried to extend ordinary factorisation into unfamiliar number systems. But a major obstacle appeared: unique factorisation, which works so naturally for ordinary integers, can fail in rings of algebraic integers. Ernst Kummer developed his theory of “ideal numbers” to recover a suitable form of factorisation in important cases. Richard Dedekind later recast and generalised these ideas into the modern notion of an ideal.

Those attempts did not produce a complete proof of Fermat’s Last Theorem. That would come much later. But the concepts developed along the way became foundational to algebraic number theory and abstract algebra.

Sometimes the ideas discovered during an unsuccessful proof are more important than the proof itself. They are “the little acorns from which mighty oaks grow”.

This is why I am, despite everything, quite excited about AI in mathematics.

I am not a mathematician. I know enough mathematics to understand a fair amount of it, and I can make my way through an introductory graduate textbook, but I remain an amateur. That may be precisely why AI has been so valuable to me. Because of it, I am learning more mathematics and doing more mathematics than before. I am thinking more, not less.

It is helping me move towards what Tao calls the *post-rigorous* stage: the point at which formal knowledge and intuition begin to reinforce one another, allowing you to see not only whether an argument works but also why it matters. Along the way, I am learning another skill: how to use AI to refine my own abilities without surrendering them. That is itself a valuable skill, and one worth mastering.

Perhaps the greatest benefit, however, is that AI helps me with distillation.

A chemist uses a separating column and condenser to isolate the different compounds in a mixture. A scientist does something similar when studying a phenomenon. The scientist’s training and mind form a kind of intellectual distillation apparatus, separating the essential from the incidental and isolating the structures hidden within experience.

Mathematics is an important component of my own apparatus. It gives me a language for extracting structure from the world. Throughout history, new encounters between mathematics and external problems have generated new concepts. Gauss’s work in geodesy and cartography, for example, helped connect the measurement of curved surfaces with the development of differential geometry.

AI is accelerating this process for me. It allows me to move more quickly between a phenomenon, its possible mathematical descriptions, and the wider body of ideas to which it might belong.

Perhaps this will happen more broadly. AI may bring into mathematics a flood of questions, intuitions, and concepts arising from domains that mathematicians previously lacked the time—or the inclination—to explore. That influx of new concepts may ultimately be more exciting than the automated production of proofs.

These are only my first impressions. I am trying to capture my own *mindscape*—my thoughts and feelings during a fleeting moment in history—much as the Impressionists tried to capture a moment of natural light falling across a landscape. Their aim was not to reproduce every detail faithfully, but to preserve an experience through colour and brushwork before it disappeared.

There is something poetic about that comparison, if you will indulge me.

Impressionism arose partly in response to photography. At first, photography appeared to threaten the painter’s purpose: why paint a scene when a machine could reproduce it more accurately? Yet photography did not bring painting to an end. It freed painters from the obligation to reproduce reality faithfully. They could instead explore perception, movement, colour, atmosphere, abstraction, and the fleeting character of experience.

A technology that seemed to automate the artist’s work expanded the meaning of art.

I wonder what AI will free mathematicians from.

More importantly, I wonder what it will inspire them to see.
